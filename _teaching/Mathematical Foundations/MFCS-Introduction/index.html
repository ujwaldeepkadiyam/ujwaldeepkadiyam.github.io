Mathematical Foundations of Computer Science

Course Introduction

Welcome to the Mathematical Foundations of Computer Science lecture series! This course provides the essential mathematical building blocks that every computer science student must master to understand algorithms, logic, automata, data structures, and computation theory at a deeper level.

The journey begins with Mathematical Logic, the formal language of reasoning used to verify programs, design circuits, build compilers, and develop intelligent systems. You will learn to represent complex statements using connectives, evaluate truth systematically, convert expressions into normal forms, and apply inference rules to derive valid conclusions. The unit also introduces Predicate Calculus, which expands logical reasoning to objects, properties, relations, and quantified statements—crucial for database theory, AI, and formal verification.

Next, we explore Sets, Relations, Functions, and Algebraic Structures, the core discrete structures used throughout computer science. From understanding binary relations, equivalence classes, partial orders, and Hasse diagrams to mastering functions, lattices, and pigeonhole principles, this unit shapes your ability to model data, classify information, and reason about structure. You will also study algebraic systems such as semigroups, monoids, and groups—important in areas like cryptography, coding theory, and automata.

Building from this foundation, the course moves into Combinatorics, the art and science of counting. Here, you will learn to compute permutations, combinations, repeated selections, and constrained arrangements. This unit also includes the Binomial and Multinomial Theorems and the Principle of Inclusion–Exclusion, which form the backbone of many combinatorial arguments used in algorithm analysis, probability theory, and discrete optimization.

The fourth unit introduces Recurrence Relations and Generating Functions, which are powerful tools for analyzing recursively defined processes. You will learn to formulate recurrences, solve them using substitution, characteristic roots, and generating functions, and apply these techniques to classical computer science problems such as divide-and-conquer algorithms, counting sequences, and combinatorial structures.

The course concludes with Graph Theory, one of the most influential subjects in computer science. You will learn various graph representations, study tree and spanning tree structures, analyze traversal algorithms like BFS and DFS, and understand the principles behind minimum spanning tree algorithms such as Kruskal’s. The unit also explores planar graphs, Eulerian and Hamiltonian paths, graph isomorphism, chromatic numbers, and practical applications of graphs in networking, social structures, scheduling, and optimization.

By the end of this course, you will have built a strong mathematical foundation that supports higher-level subjects such as algorithms, machine learning, artificial intelligence, cryptography, formal languages, automata theory, and advanced computer science research. These lecture notes will guide you step-by-step through each concept, blending intuitive explanations with rigorous definitions, proofs, and problem-solving practice.

The concepts in Mathematical Foundations of Computer Science have powerful and far-reaching applications across modern computing. Logical reasoning and predicate calculus form the backbone of compiler design, automated theorem proving, program verification, and AI reasoning engines. Sets, relations, and functions underpin database design, state machines, formal languages, and functional programming. Combinatorics and recurrence relations are essential in algorithm analysis, machine learning model design, complexity theory, probabilistic reasoning, and optimization. Algebraic structures such as groups and monoids play a key role in cryptography, coding theory, error-correcting codes, secure communication, and hashing algorithms. Graph theory powers network routing, social network analysis, search engines, recommendation systems, circuit design, scheduling, and pathfinding algorithms. Together, these topics form the mathematical backbone that enables breakthroughs in machine learning, blockchain security, distributed systems, quantum computing, and large-scale computational systems.
